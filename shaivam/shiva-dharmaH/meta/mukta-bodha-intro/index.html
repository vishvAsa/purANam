Mukta bodha intro

Emerging sometime in the middle of the sixth century, the Śivadharmaśāstra represents a non-Brāhmaṇa tradition of dharmaśāstra that provides the foundation for many common features defining Śaiva identity. It is the text that introduces Sanskrit literature to the narrative of the manifestation of liṅgodbhavamūrti, to the centrality of the mantra Oṃ namaḥ Śivāya to Śaiva tradition, and that for the first time equates the regularized installation and collective veneration of the Śivaliṅga with the defining features of the now-familiar Śaiva social and institutional worlds it vividly imagines into being. The Śivadharmaśāstra proclaims that its very essence (sāra) is Śivabhakti. As taught here directly by Śiva himself, bhakti involves a multidimensional eightfold practice of 1) tenderness towards Śiva’s devotees, 2) delight in watching others perform pūjā, 3) one’s own worship, 4) arcanā with bhakti for one’s own sake and bodily effort for the sake of devotion, 5) bhakti when listening to Śiva’s stories, 6) spontaneous transformations of the limbs and eyes and the production of sounds as a consequence of bhakti, 7) constant recollection of Śiva, and 8) a livelihood dependent upon Śiva. In contrast to the difficult, expensive, and restrictive rituals of the Veda, bhakti as taught by the Śivadharmaśāstra is represented as an easeful means (sukhopāya) accessible to all human beings regardless of social status, that bestows both spiritual and concrete material benefit, facilitates self-deification in a manner that has real-life consequences, and ultimately makes possible freedom from saṃsāra. As understood by its many centuries of interpreters, this Śivadharma serves either to mitigate or entirely nullify matters of varṇa and jāti, substituting in their place a fervent commitment to upholding Śaiva norms.

In a manner that has been barely recognized, both textual resources and models of what it means to be a Śaiva drawn from the Śivadharmaśāstra form the foundation not only of most forms of post-Gupta Śaiva textual traditions in both Sanskrit and the vernaculars, but also of the texts of the Mantramārga, except perhaps in its earliest sources (such as the Siddhayogeśvarīmata, portions of the Niśvāsa corpus, and the Pauṣkarapārameśvara). Whether critiquing what they saw as the limitations of its naïve inclusivism, as the Picumatabrahmayāmala does in its opening frame story, or supplementing its ācāra with further additions and refinements, as we find in both the Svacchandatantra and the Mataṅgapārameśvara, the teachings of the Śivadharmaśāstra, as well as its slightly younger and distinctive counterpart, the Śivadharmottara, are taken to be the baseline framework for thinking about Śaiva identity and culture. Both the Śivadharmaśāstra and the Śivadharmottara continued to be translated, transcreated, and commented upon throughout the early medieval and early modern periods. While the Śivadharmottara’s translation into Tamil has been the subject of recent publications, most of the Sanskrit, Kannada, Telugu, and Marathi literary and theological reception—with numerous unpublished commentaries and substantial reflections in the epigraphical record associated with Kālamukha, Vīramāheśvara, and Vīraśaiva traditions—has yet to be explored. Another important aspect of the Śivadharmaśāstra tradition that has received serious study is the emergence in Nepal of a series of supplemental texts, such as the Umāmaheśvarasaṃvāda, many of which incorporate elements from Vaiṣṇava traditions. MIRI aims to provide scholars access to this uncharted wealth of textual traditions.
